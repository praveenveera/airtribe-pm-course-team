# Project 4 — Competitor Analysis for Zepto AOV

## Decision question

What can Zepto learn from competing quick-commerce products and substitute shopping methods about growing baskets without assuming that more items, more discounts, or more categories automatically create valuable AOV?

## Competitor set

| Group | Products | Why included |
|---|---|---|
| Direct quick-commerce | Blinkit, Swiggy Instamart, bbnow | Compete for urgent and planned grocery missions with fast delivery. |
| Primary product | Zepto | The product being improved; use the same comparison dimensions for a fair baseline. |
| Substitutes | Supermarkets, local stores/markets, bulk buying, subscriptions | Compete for larger, planned, value-led, or recurring baskets. |

## Comparative matrix

| Product | Core promise / entry point | Assortment and mission coverage | AOV-relevant mechanism visible in public evidence | What remains unknown |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Zepto** | Fast access to everyday needs with categories, offers, and add-to-cart shopping. | Grocery, fresh, pantry, snacks, beverages, bakery, and broader everyday categories; Zepto also positions itself beyond grocery. | Category browsing, visible discounts, ratings, broad household coverage, and possible cross-mission discovery. | Whether suggestions are relevant, whether users plan larger baskets, and what causes checkout stopping. |
| **Blinkit** | Hyperlocal convenience and fast delivery. | Blinkit states 7,000+ products across grocery, fresh, bakery, meat/seafood, cosmetics, electronics, baby care, and more. | Breadth and neighbourhood-level assortment are explicit levers; company material links assortment expansion with AOV growth. | Whether breadth reduces search effort or creates choice overload; local availability and profitability are unknown. |

## Competitive patterns

### 1. Assortment is a common growth lever, not a differentiator by itself

Every direct competitor publicly promotes breadth across grocery and non-grocery categories. More catalogue alone is therefore unlikely to be a defensible Zepto strategy. The sharper question is whether the product makes the next relevant item easy to discover for the current mission.

### 2. The market serves multiple missions

The products cover urgent top-ups, planned replenishment, impulse purchases, events, household needs, and non-grocery shopping. A single “increase AOV” experience may fail because a user ordering one missing ingredient has a different job from a user doing a weekly stock-up.

### 3. Substitutes compete on basket economics

Supermarkets, local stores, bulk buying, and subscriptions may win not because they are faster, but because they offer larger packs, familiar prices, physical quality checks, or a planned routine. Quick-commerce recommendations must not push users toward smaller or worse-value packs merely to increase item count.

### 4. Promotions can grow the basket but may not grow value

Offers and thresholds are visible across quick-commerce surfaces. The project must distinguish gross order value, incremental items added, contribution margin after discounts and delivery costs, repeat purchase and trust, and items the user would have bought elsewhere anyway.

## Interim conclusion

The strongest competitive opportunity is not “add more products” or “show more discounts.” It is a mission-aware basket-building experience: help a user complete the current need, then surface a small number of relevant, good-value additions or replenishment cues without creating pressure or reducing trust.

This remains a secondary-research hypothesis. Primary research is intentionally deferred; later survey and follow-up evidence must test which missions have real headroom, what users considered but rejected, and whether price, pack size, relevance, or time caused the basket to stop.
