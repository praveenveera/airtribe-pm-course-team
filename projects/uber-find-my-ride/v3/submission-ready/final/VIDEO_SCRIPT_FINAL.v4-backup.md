# Find My Ride — final video script

**Target:** 4:20–4:40 at a calm pace. Present slides 1–10 only; the appendix remains in the PDF for evidence review.

## Slide 1 — Find My Ride

Find My Ride examines a narrow but stressful moment: after an Uber is booked, the app can show a pin, or even say the driver has arrived, while rider and driver still cannot identify a workable meeting point. The goal is not to redesign every pickup. It is to understand when coordination breaks and what Uber should test first.

## Slide 2 — What we actually researched

I want to be transparent about the method. I could not complete the required ten synchronous in-depth interviews. Instead, I ran a multilingual self-serve survey with rider and driver branches, structured tags, and optional open-text or voice prompts. After removing one confirmed duplicate, the evidence base is sixty-two unique submissions: fifty-eight riders, four drivers, thirty-three from Hyderabad, and twenty-nine from other Indian cities. This is directional primary research, not a replacement for the interview requirement.

## Slide 3 — Market and alternatives

Desk research changed the framing. Uber already publishes Hyderabad airport guidance, including in-app directions and the D1 pickup zone. Grab, Lyft, Waymo, and MyGate show patterns for venue guidance, structured handoffs, and access-aware stops. These are design precedents, not proof of local value. The opportunity is better coverage, clarity, and recovery within real access and stopping constraints.

## Slide 4 — Research goal

The research question was: when a pickup becomes difficult, what breaks, and what do people do to recover? The candidate outcome is a pickup completed without avoidable calls, location changes, extended waiting, or cancellation. Event data could strengthen this, but only if those events are captured reliably.

## Slide 5 — Evidence headline

The sample says the problem is meaningful, but not universal. Twenty-four of fifty-one recent four-wheel users reported difficulty. Among twenty-eight issue cases, twenty-three said the problem had happened before, twenty said calls or messages helped, and six said nothing helped. Importantly, “happened before” does not prove that the same venue repeatedly failed, and these percentages are not Hyderabad-wide prevalence.

## Slide 6 — Where it breaks and how people recover

The cases concentrate in complex places: airports, gated residences, malls, offices, and stations. Calling is the dominant recovery action, followed by waiting and moving. This suggests the app often hands the final physical coordination step back to rider and driver. The opportunity is to reduce that manual effort, not to assume the pickup pin is always wrong.

## Slide 7 — Strategy implication

The recommended direction is to make the fallback faster, not to make the map more complicated. Focus on selected complex locations where people already call, message, wait, or move. Avoid universal prompts, airport-only thinking, and treating every cancellation as a location failure. The order shown is a test sequence, not a committed roadmap.

## Slide 8 — Idea 1

The strongest first test is a structured pre-arrival meeting note. At flagged complex pickups, ask for one concise handoff: gate or side, landmark, and waiting spot. One respondent already does this manually. Test whether the prompt reduces reactive calls, arrived-to-trip-start dwell, location changes, and cancellations without adding friction to normal pickups.

## Slide 9 — Ideas 2 and 3

Second is guided stalled-pickup recovery. Start with reconnect actions, and test reassignment only for unresolved cases because it affects driver time and marketplace cost. Third is venue-specific coordination information at two or three validated venues. That requires ownership and expiry because stale access guidance can be worse than no guidance.

## Slide 10 — Decision and next evidence

My recommendation is to test the smallest coordination aid first, not to commit to a broad build. The evidence remains limited: the interview requirement is unmet, driver evidence is thin, and the sample is not representative. The highest-value next evidence is moderated rider and driver research, plus reliable arrived-to-trip-start and coordination-event data at selected venues. The appendix and evidence ZIP provide the full traceability.
