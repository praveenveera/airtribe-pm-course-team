# Problem Discovery, Market Research, and User Research — Part 1

> **Source boundary:** Condensed from learner-provided class notes. Numerical examples and company references are illustrative and require independent verification before reuse.

## Session at a glance

Problem discovery identifies pain worth solving. User research explains lived behaviour and motivation. Market research tests whether the opportunity is large, reachable, growing, and economically attractive. Strong product decisions triangulate these sources instead of relying on one report, interview, or metric.

## Learning objectives

- Distinguish problem discovery, user research, and market research.
- Combine primary and secondary research.
- Use TAM, SAM, SOM, CAGR, and basic sampling logic carefully.
- Separate discoverability, usage, and retention problems.
- Judge research relevance and quality.
- Map different priorities in B2B stakeholder systems.

## 1. Problem discovery

Discovery looks below the visible symptom. The goal is to understand:

- who experiences the pain;
- what they are trying to accomplish;
- where and when the friction occurs;
- how often and how severely it occurs;
- what users do today;
- whether solving it creates meaningful value.

The process is iterative:

```text
Signal → Problem hypothesis → Research → Refined problem → Opportunity decision
```

Low adoption of an impressive feature may indicate weak discovery rather than poor execution. Launch activity is not proof of durable value; continued use and retention provide stronger signals.

## 2. User research

User research provides direct evidence about behaviour, context, motivation, and friction.

### Common qualitative methods

- one-to-one interviews;
- group interviews;
- diary studies;
- field observation;
- usability observation.

Ask open-ended questions about real past behaviour:

- “Tell me about the last time…”
- “What did you do next?”
- “What made that difficult?”
- “How did you work around it?”

Avoid asking users to predict whether they would use an imagined feature.

## 3. Primary and secondary research

| Research type | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | Evidence collected directly for the current decision | Interviews, observation, surveys, experiments |
| Secondary | Existing material created for another purpose | Reports, reviews, forums, public data, competitor material |

Secondary research provides context and hypotheses. Primary research tests whether those patterns apply to the target user and problem.

For a student-finance product, secondary research might show digital-payment trends, while interviews reveal how students actually budget, borrow, or respond to payment friction. The decision becomes stronger when both signals agree.

## 4. Market research

Once a problem appears real, market research asks whether it is worth solving commercially.

Evaluate:

- demand and willingness to pay;
- market size and growth direction;
- competitive intensity and substitutes;
- saturation and room for a new position;
- distribution and acquisition economics;
- regulatory or infrastructure constraints;
- the organisation's ability to win.

A large market can still be unattractive if it is shrinking, expensive to enter, or poorly matched to the organisation's capabilities.

## 5. TAM, SAM, and SOM

- **TAM — Total Addressable Market:** the full theoretical demand.
- **SAM — Serviceable Available Market:** the part the product can serve given scope, segment, geography, and constraints.
- **SOM — Serviceable Obtainable Market:** the realistic share the organisation might capture.

Do not present a headline TAM as expected revenue. Every narrowing step should state its assumptions and source.

A vegetarian food-ordering example showed how a broad food market becomes narrower through geography, diet, ordering behaviour, reach, and realistic competitive share.

## 6. CAGR and market direction

**Compound annual growth rate (CAGR)** expresses multi-year growth as one smoothed annual rate. It helps compare market direction, but it hides year-to-year variation and depends heavily on the chosen start and end values.

Use CAGR with:

- the source and publication date;
- the measured period;
- base and final values where available;
- relevant segment and geography;
- limitations of the forecast.

## 7. Sampling and confidence

There is no universal sample size for surveys or A/B tests. Required sample size depends on:

- baseline rate;
- minimum effect worth detecting;
- desired confidence and statistical power;
- traffic and audience composition;
- decision risk.

A sample is useful only when it represents the audience and supports the decision. Small qualitative samples can reveal recurring patterns, but they do not estimate population prevalence reliably.

## 8. Competitive and build-versus-buy thinking

Competition includes direct rivals and substitutes. A late entrant needs a credible wedge such as a neglected segment, differentiated workflow, distribution advantage, or better economics.

For infrastructure such as image search, a sensible sequence may be:

1. buy or integrate a proven capability;
2. validate user value and economics;
3. build internally only if scale, control, differentiation, or cost justify it.

The choice should reflect total cost, time, strategic importance, reliability, and lock-in—not a preference to build everything.

## 9. B2B stakeholder prioritisation

The Zoom example illustrated different jobs:

| Stakeholder | Typical concern |
|---|---|
| End user | Reliable and easy communication |
| IT/admin | Security, encryption, access, and control |
| Finance/buyer | Predictable price and managed cost |

In B2B, adoption can fail if the product delights users but cannot pass security, finance, procurement, or administration gates. Identify the stakeholder who can block adoption.

## 10. Discoverability, usage, and retention

A falling usage percentage can mean different things:

- users cannot find the feature;
- new users dilute the percentage;
- users try it but do not return;
- the feature solves an infrequent job;
- the experience or outcome is weak.

Diagnose the funnel before deciding the fix. Discoverability is not the same as retention.

## 11. Research quality

Check:

- whether respondents match the intended segment;
- whether questions were neutral and relevant;
- whether the source explains its method;
- whether multiple sources show the same pattern;
- whether the finding still applies to the product context.

Reviews, Reddit, Quora, and other forums can provide useful proxy signals when access is limited, but they contain selection bias and noise.

## Practical application

For one product problem, prepare a triangulation plan:

| Evidence | Question it answers |
|---|---|
| Product analytics | Where and how often does the problem occur? |
| Interviews | Why does it happen and how do users respond? |
| Observation | What do users actually do? |
| Market sources | How large and attractive is the opportunity? |
| Competitor evidence | What alternatives and trade-offs exist? |

## Common mistakes

- Treating a technically differentiated feature as validated value.
- Using a broad market report for a narrow segment without checking relevance.
- Quoting TAM as obtainable revenue.
- Guessing sample size.
- Confusing initial curiosity with retention.
- Treating proxy sources as representative research.

## Quick reference

- Discovery finds the pain; user research explains behaviour; market research sizes the opportunity.
- Combine primary and secondary evidence.
- TAM → SAM → SOM narrows theory to realistic capture.
- Separate discoverability from retention.
- Verify the respondent, method, segment, and date.

## Questions or claims to verify

- Are the cited market reports current and relevant to the exact segment?
- What behaviour would disprove the problem hypothesis?
- Which stakeholder can block adoption even if end users like the product?
