# Fundamentals of Product Management — Part 1

> **Source boundary:** Condensed from learner-provided class notes. Examples and company references reflect classroom discussion and are not independently verified.

## Session at a glance

Product management is the discipline of solving a meaningful problem for a defined user while creating sustainable value for the business. The central lesson was to understand the problem, user, market, and business model before discussing features or technology.

## Learning objectives

By the end of the session, a learner should be able to:

- distinguish a product from a project or service;
- explain value creation and value capture;
- identify users, buyers, approvers, and other stakeholders;
- compare B2C, B2B, and B2G product environments;
- describe the PM's end-to-end ownership;
- explain how AI supports, but does not replace, PM judgement.

## 1. What product management is

A product is a repeatable and scalable way to solve a real problem, need, or opportunity for a defined user group. Product management balances three lenses:

| Lens | Main question |
|---|---|
| User | Does this solve a meaningful problem? |
| Business | Can the organisation create and capture value? |
| Technology | Can it be built and operated responsibly? |

The first questions are therefore:

1. Who is the user?
2. What job or problem do they have?
3. Why does it matter?
4. Who pays, and why?
5. Can the value be delivered repeatedly and at scale?

The solution comes after those questions. A polished interface or advanced technology cannot rescue a poorly defined problem.

## 2. Product craft and AI

Product craft is the judgement needed to define the right problem, choose useful trade-offs, and connect user value with business value. AI can accelerate research synthesis, documentation, prototyping, and analysis, but it can also make a weak premise sound convincing.

Two levels of AI fluency were discussed:

- **Using AI in PM work:** research support, drafting, prototyping, testing, and analysis.
- **Building AI products:** model behaviour, hallucination, datasets, retrieval-augmented generation, evaluation, privacy, cost, and reliability.

The PM remains accountable for the problem definition, evidence quality, decision, and outcome.

## 3. Value creation, value capture, and business models

Product value and monetisation are related but different.

- **Value creation:** the useful outcome delivered to users.
- **Value capture:** the benefit retained by the business, such as revenue, adoption, efficiency, or strategic advantage.
- **Business model:** how the organisation earns or sustains value through subscription, transaction fees, advertising, licensing, or another mechanism.

A business model cannot compensate for a product that does not solve a meaningful problem.

### Product and service

A product aims for repeatable value at scale. A service is often delivered through customised human effort. SaaS combines product characteristics with an ongoing service and billing relationship.

## 4. Markets change the product problem

| Market | Typical decision pattern | Product implication |
|---|---|---|
| B2C | User and payer are often the same | Faster feedback, high focus on convenience and retention |
| B2B | User, buyer, approver, finance, security, and procurement may differ | Multiple workflows, longer sales cycle, proof of value and risk controls |
| B2G/B2B2X | Formal procurement and approval are common | Compliance, tenders, auditability, and risk tolerance may dominate |

Multi-sided products also contain different experiences. A food-delivery platform, for example, serves customers, restaurants, delivery partners, operations teams, and internal administrators. Each group has a different job and success measure.

## 5. The PM role in practice

The PM owns the path from understanding the opportunity to measuring the result after launch. Typical responsibilities include:

- problem discovery and market understanding;
- prioritisation and roadmap decisions;
- aligning engineering, design, sales, marketing, finance, and leadership;
- clarifying requirements and trade-offs;
- monitoring delivery, adoption, and outcomes;
- learning from results and adjusting direction.

The PM usually leads through influence rather than formal authority. That requires translating the same product decision into the language each stakeholder understands.

| Stakeholder | Useful PM framing |
|---|---|
| Engineering | Scope, dependencies, reliability, and technical trade-offs |
| Design | User context, constraints, journey, and usability |
| Sales | Customer objections, qualification, and value proposition |
| Marketing | Segment, positioning, timing, and launch story |
| Finance | Revenue, cost, margin, forecast, and risk |
| Leadership | Goal, roadmap, investment, outcome, and strategic fit |

## 6. Judgement, empathy, and product sense

- **Empathy** means understanding a user's real context, not treating a broad label as one uniform segment.
- **Judgement** means choosing which problem is worth solving based on impact and expected return.
- **Product sense** combines user, business, and technology reasoning.
- **Ownership** means staying accountable across discovery, delivery, launch, adoption, and learning.

Good judgement solves the most valuable problem, not simply the loudest request.

## Examples from class

- A burger marketplace illustrated why an attractive app is insufficient without a clear segment, geography, and reason to switch.
- Zomato and Slack illustrated how user value and business value can coexist through different revenue models.
- SAP-style enterprise buying illustrated why the user, buyer, approver, and payer may be different people.
- Accessibility discussion showed why a label such as “visually impaired” is too broad without understanding different abilities and contexts.

## Practical application

Before recommending a feature, write a one-page decision frame:

1. Target user or stakeholder
2. Job or problem
3. Current alternative
4. User impact
5. Business impact
6. Evidence available
7. Key unknown
8. Success metric

## Common mistakes

- Designing for “everyone.”
- Starting with UI or technology.
- Treating the end user as the only stakeholder.
- Confusing monetisation with product value.
- Stopping at launch instead of measuring outcomes.
- Using AI output without checking the underlying assumptions.

## Quick reference

- Product = defined user + meaningful problem + repeatable value.
- Product management balances user, business, and technology.
- B2B requires stakeholder-system thinking.
- AI accelerates output; the PM owns judgement.
- PM responsibility continues after shipping.

## Questions or claims to verify

- Verify company-specific examples before reusing them in an assignment.
- Define the success metric for each stakeholder instead of assuming one product-wide metric.
- When describing an AI feature, add evaluation, privacy, cost, and failure-handling requirements.
