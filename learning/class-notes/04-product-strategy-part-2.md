# Product Strategy and Strategic Thinking — Part 2

> **Source boundary:** Condensed from learner-provided class notes. Product and company examples are teaching illustrations; verify current facts before using them as assignment evidence.

## Session at a glance

Strategy sits above execution. It turns a business goal into choices about segment, pain point, positioning, product scope, expansion, and metrics. This session focused on B2B land-and-expand strategy, consumer wedges, roadmap logic, and the parts of product management that remain human-led in an AI-enabled environment.

## Learning objectives

- Explain point-solution, platform, and land-and-expand strategies.
- Choose an initial wedge around a real user problem.
- Distinguish strategy from a feature or solution.
- Connect a business goal to a measurable roadmap.
- Explain how AI changes PM execution without removing PM accountability.
- Distinguish PM and PO responsibilities without relying only on titles.

## 1. Strategy needs research

Frameworks organise the questions; research supplies the answers. A useful sequence is:

```text
Goal → Segment → Pain point → Strategic choice → Roadmap → Metrics
```

Before deciding how to win, understand the market, user behaviour, alternatives, willingness to pay, and operational constraints.

## 2. Land and expand

**Land and expand** means entering through a narrow, valuable use case and growing into adjacent workflows after earning trust and adoption.

1. Land with one urgent job.
2. Prove value and build usage.
3. Learn the customer's wider workflow.
4. Add adjacent capabilities or integrations.
5. Expand across teams, geographies, or processes.

An employee-verification product was used as an example. It might land with one background-check workflow and later expand into onboarding, payroll setup, benefits, identity, or compliance workflows.

### Point solution versus platform

| Model | Strength | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| Point solution | Easy to explain and adopt for one urgent problem | Easier to replace and limited share of the workflow |
| Platform | Broader workflow ownership and stronger switching costs | Harder to build, sell, integrate, and operate |

Start narrow when focus and speed are essential. Move toward a platform only when customer evidence supports adjacent needs and the organisation can operate the extra complexity.

## 3. Types of expansion

- **Product expansion:** more capabilities and integrations.
- **Sales expansion:** more seats, teams, or business units.
- **Horizontal expansion:** the same product in more markets.
- **Vertical expansion:** deeper value for the existing customer base.
- **Adjacent expansion:** related offerings that broaden the workflow or market.

Expansion should improve customer value and economics, not merely increase feature count.

## 4. Consumer wedge and habit

Consumer products can also land through a narrow recurring job. Examples included health tracking, exam preparation, weight management, medicine reminders, and investing insights.

The initial wedge should answer:

- Who has the problem?
- What recurring situation triggers it?
- What outcome matters?
- Why would the user return?
- Which integration or distribution channel reduces effort?

Calendar, voice, or commerce integrations may strengthen a workflow, but only if they support the core job. Starting broad weakens the value proposition and makes habit formation harder.

## 5. Strategy is not the solution

A solution describes what is built. Strategy explains the choices behind it:

- target segment;
- important problem;
- reason to act now;
- position against alternatives;
- capability or advantage used to win;
- trade-offs and non-goals;
- measures of success.

A list of features is not a strategy. A strategy can lead to several possible solutions.

## 6. AI and the PM role

AI can accelerate:

- PRDs and documentation;
- research synthesis;
- prototypes and content;
- analytics exploration;
- competitive scans;
- communication drafts.

The PM still owns:

- deciding which problem matters;
- evaluating evidence quality;
- setting priority and timing;
- making business and ethical trade-offs;
- defining acceptable failure;
- aligning stakeholders;
- measuring the real outcome.

Faster output raises the bar for judgement. It does not remove the need for it.

## 7. Roadmaps and metrics

A roadmap should connect business goals to user and product outcomes:

1. Start with the business goal.
2. Identify the segment and pain point.
3. State the trade-off.
4. Choose the strategy.
5. Sequence outcome-oriented bets.
6. Attach metrics and learning checkpoints.

Metrics should match the product stage. Retention expectations for an early product differ from those of a mature workflow. The HEART framework—Happiness, Engagement, Adoption, Retention, and Task success—was referenced as one way to inspect experience outcomes.

## 8. PM and PO

The traditional distinction is:

| Role | Primary orientation |
|---|---|
| Product Manager | Market, customer, problem, value, and why |
| Product Owner | Delivery detail, stories, backlog, and how |

In practice, titles vary widely and many organisations combine the responsibilities. Evaluate the actual scope, decision rights, and outcomes rather than assuming the title defines the work.

## Practical application

For a B2B opportunity, create a land-and-expand map:

| Stage | Decision |
|---|---|
| Land | One urgent job, buyer, user, and proof metric |
| Adopt | Activation, workflow fit, and value evidence |
| Expand | Adjacent job, stakeholder, and commercial trigger |
| Defend | Switching cost, data, integration, or trust advantage |

## Common mistakes

- Starting with a broad platform before proving a wedge.
- Adding integrations without a recurring user job.
- Treating retention as one universal benchmark.
- Turning the roadmap into a feature calendar.
- Assuming AI-generated output is the product decision.
- Treating PM and PO titles as consistent across companies.

## Quick reference

- Land narrowly; expand after evidence of value.
- Point solutions are easier to enter with and easier to replace.
- Platforms own more workflow but carry more complexity.
- Strategy explains choices; a solution expresses one response.
- AI accelerates execution; humans remain accountable for judgement.

## Questions or claims to verify

- Which adjacent workflow do customers already ask for or solve manually?
- What adoption signal must be true before expansion?
- Does the proposed platform create real switching value or only more features?
