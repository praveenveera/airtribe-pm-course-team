# Fundamentals of Product Management — Part 2

> **Source boundary:** Condensed from learner-provided class notes. Examples and assignment guidance reflect the session notes and are not independent research evidence.

## Session at a glance

Product thinking starts with the user and the underlying need, not a requested feature. This session focused on asking better questions, finding root causes, writing useful problem statements, and developing product sense through deliberate observation and user conversations.

## Learning objectives

- Separate needs, wants, and desires.
- Move from a surface request to the underlying problem.
- Write a specific, measurable problem statement.
- Distinguish leading and lagging indicators.
- Practise product sense using real product journeys.
- Use open-ended research questions without leading the user.

## 1. Use a problem-first sequence

The basic reasoning sequence is:

1. **Who** is the user?
2. **What problem** are they trying to solve?
3. **Why now** does the problem matter?
4. **What solution** best fits the evidence and constraints?

A stated request is not always the real need. A request for a chatbot, for example, may be a symptom of slow support, failed transactions, or an unclear workflow. Product work investigates the underlying job before accepting the proposed solution.

## 2. Need, want, and desire

| Level | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Need | The underlying problem or required outcome | Reach the office |
| Want | A preferred method | Buy a car |
| Desire | A specific or aspirational preference | Buy a particular premium car |

The PM should solve the need first, then decide which wants or desires are commercially and operationally sensible.

## 3. Product sense comes from questioning

Good product thinkers reduce uncertainty before recommending an answer. In a cake-buying example, useful questions included:

- Who is the cake for?
- What is the occasion?
- How many people will attend?
- Are there dietary constraints?
- What is the budget and delivery deadline?

The lesson is transferable: clarify the user, context, constraints, and success condition before choosing the solution.

## 4. Root cause versus symptom

A surface problem describes what is visible. A root cause explains why it is happening. Fixing only the symptom can move friction elsewhere or create repeated work.

Use a simple chain:

```text
Observed outcome → Journey step → Friction → Likely cause → Evidence needed
```

Do not claim the root cause until evidence supports it. Treat it as a hypothesis and test it through data, interviews, observation, or experiments.

## 5. Good products reduce effort

Products such as WhatsApp and Amazon were discussed as examples of experiences that feel simple even when the underlying systems are complex. The goal is not visual minimalism alone. It is to reduce unnecessary cognitive and operational effort across the full task.

Review an end-to-end journey:

1. Entry and discovery
2. Evaluation
3. Decision
4. Transaction or core action
5. Confirmation
6. Support and recovery
7. Repeat use

At each step, note what is smooth, confusing, slow, or frustrating.

## 6. Core product and surrounding services

The core product performs the main job. Surrounding services help the user complete the wider workflow.

For e-commerce:

- core: discover and buy a product;
- surrounding services: payments, delivery, communication, refunds, credit, and support.

Expansion should remain connected to a clear persona and job. Practo-style appointment booking can expand into consultations, records, or pharmacy services when those adjacencies help the same health journey. Expansion without a coherent user or workflow becomes feature sprawl.

## 7. Writing problem statements

A useful problem statement contains:

- the specific user or segment;
- the situation or journey step;
- the friction or unmet need;
- the consequence for the user;
- the business impact;
- the evidence or signal;
- the boundary of the problem.

Template:

> **[User/segment]** struggles to **[job]** during **[context]** because **[observed friction]**, leading to **[user impact]** and **[business impact]**. We have observed **[evidence]** and will measure improvement through **[metric]**.

Avoid embedding a feature in the problem statement.

## 8. Leading and lagging indicators

- **Leading indicator:** an earlier behaviour or signal that can predict the outcome.
- **Lagging indicator:** the later business result.

For checkout improvement, completion of the payment step may be a leading indicator; realised revenue or repeat purchase may be a lagging outcome. Use both so the team can learn quickly without losing sight of business value.

## 9. User research

Start with open-ended questions about past behaviour:

- “Tell me about the last time you…”
- “What happened next?”
- “What was difficult?”
- “How did you solve it?”

Avoid leading questions such as “Would you use this feature?” Listen first, then form a hypothesis.

## 10. Market readiness

Even a sound idea can arrive before users, infrastructure, economics, or the surrounding ecosystem are ready. Classroom examples included early grocery delivery, Google Glass, the Humane AI Pin, and immersive devices. The general lesson is to test readiness rather than assume novelty creates adoption.

## Practical application

Choose one product you use weekly. Map the journey, record friction, speak with users, and compare their behaviour with your initial assumptions. Finish with one problem statement and one leading metric—not a feature list.

## Common mistakes

- Searching for a problem that fits a preferred solution.
- Treating a desire as a universal need.
- Using a broad persona such as “everyone.”
- Asking users to approve a feature instead of discussing past behaviour.
- Confusing a core product with every adjacent service.
- Using lagging revenue alone to diagnose a journey problem.

## Quick reference

- Who → problem → why now → solution.
- Need is the outcome; want is the method; desire is the preference.
- Diagnose root causes before designing fixes.
- Good experiences reduce effort across the full journey.
- Use leading signals for fast learning and lagging metrics for outcomes.
