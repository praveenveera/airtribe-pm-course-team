# Product Strategy and Strategic Thinking — Part 1

> **Source boundary:** Condensed from learner-provided class notes. Company examples are teaching illustrations, not independently verified strategic assessments.

## Session at a glance

Product strategy is a set of choices about where to play, how to win, how to protect the advantage, and where to invest. It starts with a specific segment and problem, connects to company goals, and becomes actionable through a roadmap and resource choices.

## Learning objectives

- Choose a segment before defining the solution.
- Distinguish vision, mission, strategy, roadmap, and backlog.
- Analyse positioning, distribution, economics, workflow, and moats.
- Recognise trade-offs and downstream effects.
- Apply systems thinking to multi-sided products.

## 1. Segment before solution

Different segments can appear to share a problem while having different motivations, willingness to pay, constraints, and success criteria.

The pet-grooming example separated:

- busy customers who value convenience; and
- older pet owners who may face mobility and trust constraints.

A good problem statement describes the pain. “Certified groomers,” “ten-minute service,” and “slot selection” are solution ideas, not problems.

## 2. Positioning

Positioning answers four practical questions:

| Question | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Who? | The target segment |
| What for? | The job, need, or category |
| Where? | Where the segment can be reached |
| Which channel? | How the product is distributed or sold |

A strong segment should be identifiable, reachable, and valuable enough to serve. Channel choices can include direct sales, app stores, marketplaces, partners, resellers, or APIs.

## 3. How to win

The session grouped competitive choices into four lenses:

| Lens | Questions |
|---|---|
| Experience | Is it easier, faster, safer, or more pleasant? |
| Economics | Can price, cost, margin, or acquisition economics work? |
| Distribution | Can the product reach users more effectively? |
| Workflow | Does it help complete the full job rather than one isolated step? |

A product does not need to win on every dimension. It needs a coherent advantage for the selected segment.

## 4. Moats and defensibility

A **unique selling proposition (USP)** explains why a user may choose the product now. A **moat** makes the advantage difficult to copy or displace over time.

Moat examples discussed included:

- distribution access;
- switching costs;
- brand trust;
- regulation or infrastructure barriers;
- cost advantage;
- network effects;
- proprietary data;
- workflow embedding;
- supply-chain control;
- partnerships.

An attractive feature is not automatically a moat. Defensibility depends on how the advantage compounds or becomes hard to replace.

## 5. Product experience versus workflow fit

- **Product experience:** how easy or pleasant the product itself feels.
- **Workflow fit:** how well it supports the user's complete task across tools, people, and steps.

A polished interface can still fail if users must leave it to complete the important work. In B2B products, workflow integration, permissions, reporting, and hand-offs may matter more than an isolated interface improvement.

## 6. Vision, mission, strategy, and roadmap

```text
Vision → Mission / goals → Strategy → Roadmap → Backlog / sprint work
```

| Layer | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Vision | Long-term aspiration |
| Mission or goals | Measurable outcomes the organisation pursues |
| Strategy | Choices about where to play and how to win |
| Roadmap | Sequenced outcomes, bets, and major initiatives |
| Backlog | Detailed delivery work |

A roadmap is not the strategy. It is the executable expression of the current strategy and should change when evidence or strategic choices change.

## 7. GTM, pricing, and resource allocation

Product strategy connects to go-to-market, pricing, and investment decisions.

- **GTM:** how the product reaches, converts, and supports its market.
- **Pricing:** how value, willingness to pay, costs, and competitive context become a commercial model.
- **Resource allocation:** which bets receive people, time, and budget.

A simple bet-sizing lens:

- **Double down:** high expected upside and high confidence.
- **Hold or optimise:** promising, but evidence is incomplete.
- **Kill or avoid:** low upside or weak strategic fit.

## 8. Trade-offs and systems thinking

Strategy requires saying no. Common trade-offs include:

- speed versus quality;
- MVP versus complete experience;
- growth versus profitability;
- standardisation versus customisation;
- short-term conversion versus long-term trust.

Systems thinking asks how a decision affects the wider ecosystem. In a marketplace, changing fees can affect demand, supply, partner economics, support load, and service quality.

- **First-order effect:** the immediate outcome.
- **Second-order effect:** a downstream consequence caused by the first change.

Map dependencies before treating a local improvement as a product-wide win.

## Examples from class

- At-home pet grooming illustrated the need to separate segments and pains.
- Instagram-style content flows illustrated experience versus complete workflow.
- An education attendance feature illustrated dependencies across teachers, students, parents, and administrators.
- A marketplace fee change illustrated first- and second-order effects.
- A Blinkit pet-salon exercise showed that strategy should begin with segment, problem, demand, and fit—not a list of features.

## Practical application

Create a one-page strategy frame:

1. Company goal
2. Target segment
3. Important problem
4. Evidence and unknowns
5. Where to play
6. How to win
7. Defensibility hypothesis
8. Trade-offs and non-goals
9. Key metric
10. First roadmap bet

## Common mistakes

- Mixing different personas in one problem statement.
- Treating a feature as the strategy.
- Confusing a USP with a durable moat.
- Creating a roadmap without explaining the strategic choice.
- Ignoring downstream users, systems, or operations.
- Avoiding explicit trade-offs.

## Quick reference

- Strategy = where to play + how to win + how to protect + where to invest.
- Segment and problem come before features.
- Roadmap turns strategy into sequenced execution.
- A moat is difficult to copy; a USP is a reason to choose.
- Assess first- and second-order effects.

## Questions or claims to verify

- Validate market size, willingness to pay, channel access, and competitive response before committing investment.
- Treat a proposed moat as a hypothesis until retention, switching behaviour, or another defensibility signal supports it.
