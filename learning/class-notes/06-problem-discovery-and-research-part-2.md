## Session at a glance

The research method should match what the team needs to learn. This session compared exploratory and evaluative research, qualitative and quantitative evidence, B2C and B2B stakeholder systems, useful personas, market sizing, Porter's Five Forces, and competitive positioning.

## Learning objectives

- Select the right research method for the decision stage.
- Combine interviews, observation, surveys, analytics, and experiments.
- Build behavioural personas rather than demographic profiles.
- Map users, buyers, approvers, champions, and blockers.
- Use Porter's Five Forces and competitor analysis carefully.
- Keep market and case scope aligned with the exact decision.

## 1. Match the method to the question

| Learning need | Suitable approach |
|---|---|
| Discover an unclear problem | Exploratory interviews, observation, diary studies |
| Explain why a known pattern occurs | Qualitative interviews and contextual observation |
| Measure frequency or scale | Surveys and analytics |
| Test whether a solution is usable | Usability testing |
| Compare two variants | A/B testing |
| Compare several combinations | Multivariate testing, with adequate traffic and design |

Do not use solution testing to pretend that problem discovery is complete. Each method answers a different question.

## 2. What each research method contributes

- **Interviews:** journeys, motivations, context, language, and hidden causes.
- **Observation:** actual behaviour, hesitation, workarounds, and environmental constraints.
- **Surveys:** directional patterns across a broader group when the likely issues are already understood.
- **Analytics:** scale, trends, cohorts, funnels, and drop-offs.
- **Session recordings or heat maps:** behavioural clues within digital experiences.
- **Experiments:** causal evidence about a defined change when design and sample size are adequate.

A strong sequence is:

```text
Analytics finds where → Qualitative research explains why → Experiment tests a response
```

## 3. B2C versus B2B research

B2C research often focuses on an individual or household. B2B research must understand a stakeholder system.

| B2B role | Core question |
|---|---|
| End user | Can I complete my job effectively? |
| Champion | Can I build internal support for this product? |
| Manager | Does this improve team outcomes? |
| IT/security | Is access, compliance, and risk acceptable? |
| Buyer/finance | Is the value worth the cost? |
| Approver/procurement | Can the organisation contract and govern it? |

Research is incomplete if it speaks only to users and ignores the people who approve, fund, administer, or block adoption.

## 4. Segments and personas

Segmentation groups people by meaningful similarities. A persona makes one segment's behaviour, motivation, goals, constraints, and context concrete.

A weak persona contains only age, gender, location, or job title. A useful persona includes:

- triggering situation;
- task or job;
- current behaviour and alternatives;
- goals and motivation;
- constraints and anxieties;
- decision criteria;
- frequency and context.

Two people with similar demographics may behave differently. Use behaviour and motivation to shape product choices.

### Research-question layers

1. **Warm-up:** factual context.
2. **Behavioural:** what the person did in a real situation.
3. **Decision-making:** why they chose, trusted, avoided, or abandoned something.

## 5. Grocery example: habit and value proposition

Grocery ordering illustrated how the same product can serve different jobs:

- fast replenishment when an item runs out;
- planned monthly basket building;
- price-conscious shopping;
- trust in freshness and availability;
- repeat ordering with less effort.

A subscription or reorder feature should appear where the recurring habit breaks. Before building it, learn how people notice depletion, create lists, compare options, and respond to substitutions.

## 6. Market sizing

TAM, SAM, and SOM form a funnel:

```text
Total demand → Reachable demand → Realistically obtainable share
```

A common top-down estimate multiplies the number of relevant customers by expected annual spend. A bottom-up estimate uses actual capacity, channels, conversion, and pricing. Record every assumption; false precision does not improve the decision.

## 7. Porter's Five Forces

The framework assesses structural pressure in a market:

1. Rivalry among existing competitors
2. Buyer power
3. Supplier power
4. Threat of substitutes
5. Threat of new entrants

A market can be large yet unattractive when rivalry, supplier power, regulation, or capital requirements make sustainable economics difficult. Aviation and food delivery were discussed as examples of markets with multiple structural pressures.

Regulation is not one of the original five forces, but it can materially shape entry, cost, and bargaining power and should be analysed explicitly in regulated markets.

## 8. Competitive analysis and positioning

Start with:

- **What** is being offered?
- **Why now** is the opportunity important?
- **How** will the product win?

Include direct competitors and substitutes. Compare dimensions that matter to the selected buyer, such as price, reliability, speed, coverage, trust, integration, or service. A feature inventory alone does not reveal strategic trade-offs.

Useful sources can include official product material, demonstrations, customer reviews, app-store feedback, analyst material, and direct user research. One- and two-star reviews can expose friction but are not representative by themselves.

## 9. Scope and case quality

A strong case defines the exact segment, transaction, geography, and decision. It includes:

- market size and assumptions;
- cost structure;
- competitive landscape;
- market direction;
- strategic fit;
- supporting evidence and limitations;
- a clear recommendation.

The purpose is not to collect the most opinions. It is to synthesise evidence into a decision.

## 10. Bias and practical judgement

Research can be distorted by small samples, wrong participants, leading questions, vague responses, dishonest answers, or random noise. Look for repeated patterns across suitable participants and methods.

Repeated qualitative patterns can support a hypothesis. They do not, on their own, prove population size or causality.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing a method before defining the research question.
- Building personas from demographics alone.
- Interviewing only end users in a B2B product.
- Treating surveys as instructions for what to build.
- Comparing competitors on irrelevant features.
- Expanding the case beyond the decision being made.

## Quick reference

- Explore when the problem is unclear; evaluate when a solution exists.
- Qualitative evidence explains why; quantitative evidence shows where and how much.
- Strong personas contain behaviour, motivation, and constraints.
- Five Forces assesses market pressure, not just named competitors.
- Keep the market definition aligned with the business question.

## Questions or claims to verify

- Does the sample contain the users and stakeholders relevant to the decision?
- Which competitor dimensions actually change buyer choice?
- Are market-sizing assumptions dated, sourced, and internally consistent?
